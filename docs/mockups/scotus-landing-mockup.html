How Does the Supreme Court Lean?

Martin–Quinn scores estimate each justice's ideology from how they vote in divided cases: justices who vote alike land close together, and a justice's position is allowed to move from one term to the next. Pick a term to see who sat on the Court, which presidents appointed them, and how far each one has moved since.

Mockup. Scores shown are illustrative trajectories, not real Martin–Quinn estimates.

Where the justices stand

The Court in the selected term, left to right. Dot color is the party of the president who appointed each justice.

Martin–Quinn scores run on a single liberal–conservative scale and are not comparable in number to the DW-NOMINATE scores on the Congress pages. Dimming a party's appointees never changes the median. In terms with a mid-term change, hollow dots mark the justice who left or joined, and the median reflects the Court after the change.

Who each president appointed

Career average for every justice a president put on the Court. A ring marks justices sitting in the selected term.

Career average is the mean of a justice's per-term scores. It can hide justices who moved a long way. Justices still serving are not final.

How each justice moved

Every justice's score, term by term, since 1937. Click or drag to change the term; click a line to follow one justice.

Scores are smoothed by the model, so a justice's stability is partly an assumption of the method. The shaded band for a selected justice is its estimation uncertainty.

Source: Martin, Andrew D. and Kevin M. Quinn. 2002. "Dynamic Ideal Point Estimation via Markov Chain Monte Carlo for the U.S. Supreme Court, 1953–1999." Political Analysis 10:134–153. Scores from mqscores.wustl.edu.
